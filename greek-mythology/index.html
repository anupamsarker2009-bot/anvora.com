Mythology
14 parts · 95 chapters

Greek Mythology

The whole story, in order: from the empty Void at the beginning of everything, through the gods, the first people and the great heroes, to the fall of Troy and the end of the Age of Heroes. About 50 minutes of reading.

Contents

From Chaos to the last of the heroes

Read it from start to finish, or jump to any part. The myths come in many versions; this retelling follows the best-known one.

I
Part I · 4 chapters

The Beginning

Before gods, before light, before anything had a shape, there was only the Void.

Chapter 1

Chaos and the first powers

In the beginning there was Chaos. The word did not mean disorder, as it does today. It meant a gap: a vast, dark, yawning emptiness with no up or down, no time and no shape.

Out of Chaos came the first great powers of the world. Gaia, the Earth, broad-breasted and solid, the firm seat of everything that would ever live. Tartarus, the deepest pit, as far below the earth as the earth is below the sky. And Eros, the force of desire, the most beautiful of the immortals, who makes things come together and so makes new things begin.

From Chaos also came Erebus, the darkness under the earth, and Nyx, the Night. Night and Darkness joined, and Night gave birth to Aether, the bright upper air, and Hemera, the Day. So light was born out of darkness, and day and night began their endless turning.

Chapter 2

Gaia’s children

Gaia, alone, gave birth to Uranus, the starry Sky, as large as herself, so that he would cover her on every side. She also bore the tall mountains, where nymphs would one day live, and Pontus, the barren, restless sea.

Then Gaia lay with Uranus, and their children were the first gods of real power. There were twelve Titans: Oceanus, the river that circles the world; Coeus and Crius; Hyperion, the high one who watches over the sun’s path; Iapetus; Theia and Rhea; Themis, divine law; Mnemosyne, memory; Phoebe; Tethys, wife of Oceanus; and last, the youngest and most cunning, Cronus.

Gaia and Uranus also had stranger children. Three Cyclopes, each with a single round eye in the middle of his forehead, master smiths named Thunder, Lightning and Bright. And three Hundred-Handers, Cottus, Briareus and Gyges, each with a hundred arms and fifty heads, so strong that nothing could stand against them.

Chapter 3

The crime of Uranus

Uranus hated his children. He was afraid of their strength. As each one was born, he pushed it back into the depths of Gaia’s body and kept it there, in the dark, and he was pleased with what he had done.

Gaia groaned under the weight of her imprisoned children. In her anger she made a new metal, grey adamant, and from it she shaped a great jagged sickle. Then she went to her children and asked which of them would dare to punish their father.

All of them were afraid except Cronus. “Mother,” he said, “I will do it. I have no respect for a father with such a cruel heart.”

Chapter 4

The sickle in the dark

Gaia hid Cronus in ambush and put the sickle in his hand. When night came, Uranus came down to lie with Gaia, spreading himself over her. Cronus reached out from his hiding place and, with one sweep of the sickle, cut off his father’s genitals and threw them behind him.

Nothing about this act was wasted. Where the drops of blood fell on the earth, Gaia gave birth to the Erinyes, the Furies, who hunt down those who harm their own family. She also bore the Giants, gleaming in armour, and the ash-tree nymphs.

The severed flesh fell into the sea and drifted for a long time. White foam gathered around it, and from the foam a girl grew. She came ashore at Cyprus, and grass sprang up under her feet. This was Aphrodite, goddess of love and beauty, born from the sea foam.

Uranus, wounded and shamed, withdrew forever. He cursed his children with the name Titans, the “strainers”, and warned that one day they would pay for what they had done.

II
Part II · 4 chapters

The Age of the Titans

Cronus took his father’s throne, and found out how hard it is to keep one.

Chapter 5

The reign of Cronus

With Uranus gone, Cronus became lord of the universe. He married his sister Rhea, and for a while the world was at peace. Later poets would remember this time as a Golden Age, when people lived without work or worry.

But Cronus did not free all of his brothers. The Cyclopes and the Hundred-Handers stayed locked away in the dark, because Cronus feared them as much as his father had.

And Cronus had a fear of his own. Gaia and Uranus had told him a prophecy: just as he had overthrown his father, he would one day be overthrown by his own son.

Chapter 6

The swallowed children

Cronus decided to make sure the prophecy could never come true. Each time Rhea gave birth, he took the baby from her and swallowed it whole.

First Hestia, then Demeter, then Hera, then Hades, then Poseidon. Five children went down into the darkness of their father’s belly, alive but trapped. Rhea’s grief grew with each one, until it was as heavy as Gaia’s had once been.

When Rhea was pregnant for the sixth time, she went to her parents, Gaia and Uranus, and begged them for a way to save this child.

Chapter 7

The stone in the blanket

Gaia and Uranus sent Rhea to Crete. There, in a cave on Mount Ida or Mount Dikte (the stories differ), she gave birth to her youngest son, Zeus, and left him in the care of Gaia and the nymphs.

Then Rhea took a large stone, wrapped it in swaddling clothes and handed it to Cronus. He took it and swallowed it without a second look.

The baby Zeus grew up in secret. He was fed on honey and on the milk of the goat Amalthea. Outside the cave, warriors called the Curetes danced and clashed their spears on their shields, so that Cronus would never hear the baby cry. Later, one of Amalthea’s horns became the Cornucopia, the horn of plenty, always full of food.

Chapter 8

Zeus frees his siblings

When Zeus was grown, he set out to take back what his father had stolen. With the help of Metis, a Titan’s daughter and the wisest of all beings, he gave Cronus a drink that made him sick.

Cronus vomited up everything he had swallowed, in reverse order. First came the stone, which Zeus set up at Delphi as a marker for all time. Then came Poseidon, Hades, Hera, Demeter and Hestia, fully grown.

The five gods stood beside their youngest brother. They owed him their freedom, and they were ready to fight.

III
Part III · 6 chapters

The Wars of the Gods

Three great wars decided who would rule the world.

Chapter 9

The Titanomachy

Zeus and his siblings made Mount Olympus their stronghold. Cronus and most of the Titans held Mount Othrys across the plain. For ten long years they fought, and neither side could win.

Not every Titan fought for Cronus. Themis and her son Prometheus sided with Zeus. So did Styx, the river goddess, who brought her children Victory, Power, Rivalry and Force, and was honoured forever after: from then on, the gods swore their most binding oaths by her waters.

Chapter 10

The weapons of victory

Gaia told Zeus how to end the war. He must free the prisoners his father and grandfather had locked away.

Zeus went down into Tartarus and released the Cyclopes and the Hundred-Handers. In gratitude, the Cyclopes forged three gifts. For Zeus, the thunderbolt. For Poseidon, the trident, which could shake the earth. For Hades, a helmet that made its wearer invisible.

The last battle shook the universe. Zeus hurled thunderbolt after thunderbolt, and the forests burned and the sea boiled. The Hundred-Handers threw boulders three hundred at a time, until the Titans were buried under a cloud of stone.

Chapter 11

The fate of the Titans

The defeated Titans were thrown into Tartarus and locked behind bronze gates, with the Hundred-Handers as their guards.

Atlas, who had led the Titans in battle, received a special punishment. He was sent to the western edge of the world to hold up the sky on his shoulders forever.

Some Titans who had not fought were left in peace. Oceanus kept his river, Helios kept driving the sun, and Themis and Mnemosyne became honoured companions of the new king.

Chapter 12

Dividing the world

The three sons of Cronus drew lots for the world. Zeus won the sky and became king of gods and men. Poseidon won the sea. Hades won the underworld, the kingdom of the dead.

The earth itself, and the heights of Olympus, they agreed to share. Of the sisters, Hestia took the hearth at the centre of every home, Demeter the grain and the harvest, and Hera became queen of the gods.

Chapter 13

The Gigantomachy

Gaia was not happy. Her children the Titans were locked in Tartarus, and so she called on her other children, the Giants, born from the blood of Uranus. They piled mountains on top of each other to climb to Olympus, and they hurled burning trees and rocks at the gods.

A prophecy said that the gods could only win with the help of a mortal. So Zeus called on his son Heracles. The gods would strike the Giants down, and Heracles finished them with his arrows.

Athena threw the island of Sicily on top of the Giant Enceladus. He is still there, under Mount Etna, and the volcano’s fire is his breath.

Chapter 14

Typhon, the last challenger

Gaia made one final attempt. With Tartarus she produced Typhon, the most terrible monster ever born. His head brushed the stars, his arms stretched from east to west, and a hundred snake heads grew from his shoulders, hissing and roaring with every voice there is.

Most of the gods fled in terror to Egypt, disguised as animals. Zeus alone stood and fought. At first Typhon won: he tore out Zeus’s sinews and hid them in a cave. Hermes and Pan stole them back, and Zeus, made whole again, chased Typhon across the world with his thunderbolts.

At last Zeus buried Typhon under Mount Etna, or in Tartarus. From Typhon come the wild, destructive winds. After this, no one challenged the rule of Zeus again.

IV
Part IV · 6 chapters

The Olympians

The new rulers made their home on the highest mountain in Greece.

Chapter 15

The wives of Zeus

Zeus’s first wife was Metis, the wisest of the immortals. But a prophecy warned that she would bear a son who would overthrow him. So Zeus did what his father had done, with a twist: he tricked Metis into turning into a fly and swallowed her. From then on, her wisdom lived inside him.

Metis was already pregnant. Some time later, Zeus had a crushing headache. Hephaestus (or in some versions Prometheus) split his head open with an axe, and out sprang Athena, fully grown and in full armour, shouting a war cry. She became goddess of wisdom, crafts and strategy, and her father’s favourite.

Zeus then married Themis, who bore the Seasons and the three Fates, who spin, measure and cut the thread of every life. He had the nine Muses with Mnemosyne, the three Graces with Eurynome, and Persephone with Demeter. His last and lasting wife was his sister Hera.

Chapter 16

The twelve of Olympus

Twelve great gods made their home on Olympus, in palaces built by Hephaestus. Hades, though one of the most powerful, lived below in his own kingdom and was not counted among them.

Chapter 17

Hera and the children of Zeus

Hera was queen of heaven and goddess of marriage, and her marriage was not a happy one. Zeus pursued goddesses, nymphs and mortal women again and again, and Hera’s jealousy fell hard on his lovers and their children.

Hera had children of her own. Ares, the god of war, whom even his father disliked. Hebe, goddess of youth, who poured the gods’ nectar. Eileithyia, goddess of childbirth. And, in some tellings by herself alone, Hephaestus.

Chapter 18

The fall of Hephaestus

Hephaestus was born lame, and Hera, ashamed, threw him from Olympus. He fell into the sea and was raised by the sea goddesses Thetis and Eurynome, in an underwater cave where he learned to work metal.

He took his revenge with a gift: a beautiful golden throne for his mother. When Hera sat in it, invisible chains held her fast, and no god could free her. Only Dionysus could bring Hephaestus back to Olympus, by getting him drunk and leading him up the mountain on a donkey. Hephaestus released his mother, and in return he was given Aphrodite as his wife.

Chapter 19

Aphrodite and Ares

Aphrodite did not love her lame, hard-working husband. She loved Ares. Helios, the Sun, who sees everything, saw them together and told Hephaestus.

Hephaestus forged a net of chains finer than spider’s silk and hung it around his bed. When the lovers next lay there, the net dropped and trapped them. Hephaestus called all the gods to come and look. The goddesses stayed away out of modesty, but the gods came, and their laughter, Homer tells us, could not be stopped.

Chapter 20

Persephone and the seasons

Demeter, goddess of the harvest, had a daughter, Persephone, whom she loved above everything. One day, as Persephone was picking flowers in a meadow, the earth split open. Hades rode out in his chariot, seized her and carried her down to the underworld to be his queen. Zeus had agreed to it, but no one had asked Demeter.

Demeter searched the world with burning torches for nine days. When she learned the truth, she left Olympus in grief and anger, and nothing grew. The crops failed, people starved, and the gods received no offerings.

Zeus finally sent Hermes to bring Persephone back. But in the underworld she had eaten a few seeds of a pomegranate, and anyone who eats the food of the dead must return to them. So a bargain was made: Persephone would spend part of every year with Hades, and the rest with her mother.

When Persephone comes up from below, Demeter is glad and the earth blooms. When she goes down again, Demeter mourns, and winter comes.

V
Part V · 8 chapters

The Making of Mankind

Humans were shaped from clay, given fire, and handed a jar they should never have opened.

Chapter 21

Prometheus shapes the first people

Prometheus, whose name means “forethought”, was the son of the Titan Iapetus. He had fought on Zeus’s side, but his loyalty was always to the creatures he loved most: human beings.

In many tellings it was Prometheus who made them. He took clay, mixed it with water and shaped figures in the image of the gods, standing upright and looking at the sky. His brother Epimetheus, “afterthought”, had been given the job of handing out gifts to all the animals: speed, claws, wings, fur. By the time he reached humans, there was nothing left. They were naked, weak and cold.

Chapter 22

The trick at Mecone

At Mecone, gods and humans met to decide how sacrifices would be shared. Prometheus killed an ox and made two portions. In one, he hid the good meat inside the ox’s ugly stomach. In the other, he wrapped the bare bones in shiny, tempting fat.

He asked Zeus to choose. Zeus picked the fat, and found only bones. From then on, people burned the bones for the gods and kept the meat for themselves. But Zeus was furious, and to punish humans he took fire away from them.

Chapter 23

The theft of fire

Prometheus would not let his people freeze in the dark. He climbed to Olympus, took a spark of fire and hid it in the hollow stalk of a fennel plant. Then he carried it down to earth.

With fire, humans could cook, keep warm, work metal and build. Prometheus also taught them to count, to write, to read the stars, to tame animals, to heal and to sail. Civilisation began with his stolen spark.

Chapter 24

The punishment of Prometheus

Zeus had Prometheus chained to a rock in the Caucasus Mountains, at the edge of the world. Every day an eagle flew down and ate his liver, and every night the liver grew back, because Prometheus was immortal.

He suffered for many generations. Prometheus knew a secret that Zeus needed, about which goddess would bear a son greater than his father, and he refused to tell it. In the end, Heracles killed the eagle and set him free, and Prometheus revealed the secret: it was the sea goddess Thetis. Zeus never married her, and her son became the hero Achilles.

Chapter 25

Pandora’s jar

Zeus had one more punishment for humans. He ordered Hephaestus to make a woman out of earth and water. Each god gave her a gift: beauty from Aphrodite, skill from Athena, and from Hermes, a clever tongue and a curious, deceitful mind. She was named Pandora, “all-gifted”.

Zeus sent her to Epimetheus, who had been warned by his brother never to accept a gift from Zeus. He forgot the warning and married her. Pandora brought a jar with her (later stories made it a box). One day she lifted the lid.

Out flew every evil that humans had never known: sickness, hard work, old age, grief and death. They spread across the earth. Pandora slammed the lid down, but it was too late. Only one thing was left inside, caught under the rim: Hope.

Chapter 26

The Five Ages of Man

The poet Hesiod told the story of humanity as a series of five ages, each worse than the one before.

First came the Golden Age, under Cronus, when people lived like gods, never grew old and died as if falling asleep. Then the Silver Age, when people stayed foolish children for a hundred years and refused to honour the gods, so Zeus put an end to them.

Next came the Bronze Age, a race of violent warriors with bronze weapons and bronze houses, who destroyed each other. After them came the Age of Heroes, the demigods who fought at Thebes and Troy, nobler than the bronze race. Last came our own Age of Iron, full of hard work and sorrow, which Zeus will one day destroy as well.

Chapter 27

The Flood

In the Bronze Age, people became so wicked that Zeus decided to wipe them out. He went down to earth in disguise to test them, and visited King Lycaon of Arcadia. Lycaon served him a meal of human flesh. Zeus turned him into a wolf and returned to Olympus resolved to drown the world.

Prometheus warned his son Deucalion, who built a chest, or an ark, and filled it with supplies. Then the rain came. For nine days and nights the waters rose until only the peaks of the highest mountains showed above them. Everyone else drowned.

Deucalion and his wife Pyrrha, the daughter of Pandora, came to rest on Mount Parnassus. They were the only people left alive.

Chapter 28

The stones that became people

Deucalion and Pyrrha went to the oracle of Themis and asked how they could bring back the human race. The answer: “Throw the bones of your mother behind you.”

At first Pyrrha was horrified. Then Deucalion understood. Their mother was Gaia, the Earth, and her bones were the stones. They walked on, throwing stones over their shoulders without looking back. The stones Deucalion threw became men. The stones Pyrrha threw became women.

Their son Hellen gave his name to the Hellenes, as the Greeks still call themselves. His sons and grandsons became the ancestors of the Greek peoples: Dorians, Aeolians, Ionians and Achaeans.

VI
Part VI · 7 chapters

Gods Among Mortals

As the world filled with people again, the gods could not leave them alone.

Chapter 29

Io, the wandering heifer

Io was a priestess of Hera at Argos. Zeus fell in love with her, and to hide her from his wife he turned her into a beautiful white heifer. Hera was not fooled. She asked for the heifer as a gift, and set Argus, a giant with a hundred eyes, to guard her. Argus never slept with all his eyes at once.

Zeus sent Hermes, who played the pipes and told long stories until every one of Argus’s eyes closed, and then killed him. Hera put the hundred eyes on the tail of her bird, the peacock.

Then Hera sent a gadfly to sting Io, and she ran in agony across the world. The sea she swam across is still called the Ionian Sea, and the strait she crossed is the Bosporus, the “cow’s crossing”. At last she reached Egypt, where Zeus turned her back into a woman. Her descendants would include some of the greatest heroes of Greece.

Chapter 30

Europa and the bull

Europa was a princess of Phoenicia. One day, as she played by the shore, she saw a beautiful white bull grazing among her father’s cattle. It was so gentle that she garlanded its horns with flowers and climbed onto its back.

The bull was Zeus. He walked into the sea and swam away with her, all the way to Crete. There Europa bore him three sons, among them Minos, who became the great king of Crete. The continent of Europe still bears her name.

Her brother Cadmus was sent to search for her, and told not to come home without her. He never found her, but his search would lead him to found a city.

Chapter 31

Phaethon and the chariot of the sun

Phaethon was the son of Helios, the Sun. When other boys mocked him and said he had no divine father, he travelled to the palace of the Sun to find out the truth. Helios welcomed him and swore by the river Styx to give him anything he asked.

Phaethon asked to drive the chariot of the sun for one day. Helios begged him to choose anything else, but an oath by the Styx cannot be broken.

The fiery horses felt the light hand on the reins and bolted. The chariot swerved too high, and the earth froze. Then it plunged too low, and the earth burned: rivers dried up, forests caught fire, and the people of Africa, the story says, were scorched dark. To save the world, Zeus struck Phaethon down with a thunderbolt. He fell like a shooting star into the river Eridanus, and his sisters, weeping on the bank, turned into poplar trees whose tears became amber.

Chapter 32

Arachne the weaver

Arachne was a young woman from Lydia who wove more beautifully than anyone alive. People said she must have been taught by Athena herself. Arachne said no: she was better than Athena, and would prove it.

Athena came to her disguised as an old woman and warned her to show respect. Arachne refused. So the goddess revealed herself, and the contest began.

Athena wove the gods in all their majesty, and in the corners, warnings of mortals punished for their pride. Arachne wove the gods’ scandals: Zeus as a bull, a swan, a shower of gold. Her work was flawless. In a rage, Athena tore the tapestry apart and struck her. Arachne, in despair, hanged herself. Athena turned her into a spider, so that she and her descendants would hang and weave forever.

Chapter 33

Niobe’s tears

Niobe, queen of Thebes, had fourteen children, seven sons and seven daughters, and she was proud of them. She boasted that she was greater than the goddess Leto, who had only two children.

Those two children were Apollo and Artemis. To avenge their mother, Apollo shot all of Niobe’s sons with his arrows, and Artemis shot all her daughters. Niobe wept so long that she turned to stone. On Mount Sipylus there is a rock that looks like a weeping woman, and water runs down it all year long.

Chapter 34

Echo and Narcissus

Echo was a nymph who talked too much. She distracted Hera with chatter while Zeus slipped away, and Hera punished her: from then on, she could only repeat the last words other people said.

Echo fell in love with Narcissus, a young man so beautiful that everyone wanted him, and who wanted no one. When she ran to embrace him, he pushed her away. She faded away in the mountains until only her voice was left.

The gods heard the prayers of those Narcissus had rejected. He bent over a still pool to drink, saw his own reflection and fell in love with it. He could not leave it, and wasted away beside the water. Where he died, a flower grew: the narcissus.

Chapter 35

Tantalus and Sisyphus

Tantalus was a son of Zeus, so honoured that he ate with the gods. He abused their trust. He stole nectar and ambrosia, and, to test whether the gods knew everything, he killed his own son Pelops, cooked him and served him to them.

The gods knew. They brought Pelops back to life, and sent Tantalus to Tartarus. He stands in a pool of water under a fruit tree. When he bends to drink, the water drains away. When he reaches for the fruit, the wind lifts the branch out of reach. From his name comes the word “tantalise”.

Sisyphus, king of Corinth, was the cleverest of mortals. When Death came for him, Sisyphus chained Death up, and for a while no one could die. When he finally did die, he tricked his way out of the underworld and lived to old age. His punishment in Tartarus was to roll a huge boulder up a hill. Every time it nears the top, it rolls back down, and he must start again, forever.

VII
Part VII · 6 chapters

The First Heroes

The Age of Heroes began with a son of Zeus, a flying horse and a monster with snakes for hair.

Chapter 36

Danae and the shower of gold

King Acrisius of Argos was told by an oracle that his daughter’s son would kill him. So he locked his daughter Danae in a bronze chamber under the ground, where no man could reach her.

But Zeus could. He came to her as a shower of gold, falling through the roof, and Danae gave birth to a son, Perseus. Acrisius did not dare kill his grandson outright. He shut mother and baby in a wooden chest and threw it into the sea.

The chest washed ashore on the island of Seriphos, where a fisherman named Dictys took them in. Perseus grew up there into a strong young man.

Chapter 37

The quest for Medusa

Dictys’s brother, Polydectes, was king of Seriphos. He wanted Danae, and Perseus stood in his way. So he held a feast where every guest had to bring a gift. Perseus, who had nothing, boasted that he would bring anything the king asked for, even the head of Medusa.

Medusa was one of the three Gorgons. She had snakes for hair, and anyone who looked at her face turned to stone.

The gods helped Perseus. Athena gave him a polished bronze shield and Hermes a curved sword of adamant. From the three Graeae, old women who shared one eye and one tooth between them, he learned the way to the nymphs, by stealing their eye until they told him. The nymphs gave him winged sandals, a bag to carry the head in, and the helmet of Hades that made him invisible.

Chapter 38

The head of the Gorgon

Perseus flew to the edge of the world and found the Gorgons asleep. He did not look at Medusa directly. He watched her reflection in Athena’s shield, and with one stroke cut off her head and stuffed it into the bag.

From Medusa’s neck sprang two children by Poseidon: Pegasus, the winged horse, and the warrior Chrysaor. Her sisters woke and chased Perseus, but in Hades’ helmet he could not be seen.

Chapter 39

Andromeda

On his way home, Perseus flew over Ethiopia and saw a young woman chained to a rock by the sea. This was Princess Andromeda. Her mother, Queen Cassiopeia, had boasted that she was more beautiful than the sea nymphs, and Poseidon had sent a sea monster to ravage the coast. The only way to stop it was to sacrifice Andromeda.

Perseus offered to save her if he could marry her. When the monster rose from the waves, he swooped down and killed it. At the wedding feast, Andromeda’s uncle, who had been promised her first, burst in with armed men. Perseus pulled out Medusa’s head and turned them all to stone.

Chapter 40

The prophecy fulfilled

Back on Seriphos, Perseus found his mother hiding from Polydectes. He walked into the king’s hall and said he had brought the gift. Then he held up the head, and Polydectes and his court turned to stone. Perseus made the kind fisherman Dictys king, and gave Medusa’s head to Athena, who set it on her shield.

Later, at athletic games in the city of Larissa, Perseus threw a discus. The wind caught it, and it struck an old man in the crowd and killed him. It was his grandfather, Acrisius, who had come there in fear of the prophecy. No one escapes what the Fates have spun.

Perseus and Andromeda founded the city of Mycenae, and among their grandchildren was Alcmena, the mother of Heracles. In the end, Perseus, Andromeda, Cassiopeia and the sea monster were all placed in the sky as constellations.

Chapter 41

Bellerophon and Pegasus

Bellerophon, a prince of Corinth, wanted to tame the winged horse Pegasus. Athena came to him in a dream and gave him a golden bridle. When he woke, the bridle was beside him, and with it he caught Pegasus as the horse drank from a spring.

Bellerophon was sent to Lycia with a sealed letter that asked the king to kill him, after a queen falsely accused him. The king did not want blood on his hands, so instead he sent Bellerophon to kill the Chimera, a fire-breathing monster with the head of a lion, the body of a goat and a snake for a tail. Flying on Pegasus, Bellerophon shot it from above, and drove a lump of lead into its jaws, which melted in its fiery breath and killed it.

He survived every other task too, married the king’s daughter and was honoured as a hero. But success made him proud. He tried to ride Pegasus up to Olympus to join the gods. Zeus sent a gadfly to sting the horse, and Bellerophon fell to earth. He wandered alone, lame and hated by the gods, for the rest of his life. Pegasus flew on to Olympus and carried Zeus’s thunderbolts.

VIII
Part VIII · 4 chapters

Heracles

The greatest of all Greek heroes was born to suffer, and to win.

Chapter 42

The birth of Heracles

Alcmena, granddaughter of Perseus, was married to Amphitryon. While Amphitryon was away at war, Zeus came to her disguised as her husband, and made the night last three times as long as usual. Alcmena gave birth to twins: Heracles, son of Zeus, and Iphicles, son of Amphitryon.

Hera hated Heracles from the start. His name means “glory of Hera”, perhaps in the hope of calming her. When he was eight months old, she sent two great snakes into the twins’ room. Iphicles screamed. Heracles grabbed a snake in each hand and strangled them, and was found laughing, holding them up like toys.

Chapter 43

Madness and the oracle

Heracles grew up enormously strong. He was taught by the best: archery, wrestling, chariot-driving and, less successfully, music. He killed his music teacher with a lyre when he was punished for a mistake.

As a young man he won a war for the king of Thebes and married the princess Megara. They had children and were happy. Then Hera sent madness upon him. Heracles did not know what he was doing, and he killed his own children. When his mind cleared and he saw what he had done, he wanted to die.

He went to the oracle at Delphi to ask how he could be purified. The answer: he must serve his cousin Eurystheus, king of Tiryns, for twelve years, and do whatever labours he set.

Chapter 44

The Twelve Labours

Eurystheus was a cowardly man who hid in a bronze jar whenever Heracles came back with a monster. He set tasks that were meant to be impossible, and Heracles completed every one.

  1. 01

    The Nemean Lion

    Its golden hide could not be cut by any weapon, so Heracles strangled it, and wore its skin as armour ever after.

  2. 02

    The Lernaean Hydra

    A swamp serpent with many heads. For every head cut off, two grew back, until his nephew Iolaus burned each neck shut.

  3. 03

    The Ceryneian Hind

    A golden-horned deer sacred to Artemis. Heracles chased it for a whole year before catching it alive.

  4. 04

    The Erymanthian Boar

    He drove the giant boar into deep snow, trapped it in a net and carried it back alive.

  5. 05

    The Augean Stables

    Thirty years of dung from thousands of cattle, cleaned in a single day by turning two rivers through the stables.

  6. 06

    The Stymphalian Birds

    Man-eating birds with bronze feathers. He scared them into the air with a bronze rattle and shot them down.

  7. 07

    The Cretan Bull

    The bull from the sea that fathered the Minotaur. He wrestled it and shipped it back to Greece.

  8. 08

    The Mares of Diomedes

    Horses that ate human flesh. He fed them their cruel master, and they became tame.

  9. 09

    The Belt of Hippolyta

    The war belt of the Amazon queen. She agreed to give it to him, but Hera stirred up a battle.

  10. 10

    The Cattle of Geryon

    Red cattle owned by a three-bodied giant at the western edge of the world. On the way he set up the Pillars of Heracles.

  11. 11

    The Apples of the Hesperides

    He held up the sky while Atlas fetched the golden apples, then tricked Atlas into taking it back.

  12. 12

    Cerberus

    The last labour: he went down to the underworld and carried out its three-headed guard dog with his bare hands.

Chapter 45

The death of Heracles

After the labours, Heracles’ life was still full of adventures. He sailed with the Argonauts for a time, fought the Giants beside the gods, and freed Prometheus from his chains. He married Deianira, a princess of Calydon.

Once, as they crossed a river, the centaur Nessus offered to carry Deianira over, and then tried to carry her off. Heracles shot him with an arrow dipped in the Hydra’s poison. As he died, Nessus told Deianira to keep some of his blood: if Heracles ever stopped loving her, it would win him back.

Years later, Deianira heard that Heracles had fallen in love with a young captive, Iole. She soaked a robe in the centaur’s blood and sent it to him. When Heracles put it on, the poison burned into his skin. He could not tear it off without tearing his flesh away.

In agony, Heracles built his own funeral pyre on Mount Oeta and climbed onto it. No one would light it, until the young Philoctetes did, and Heracles gave him his bow in return. As the flames rose, there was a crash of thunder. Heracles’ mortal part burned away, and his immortal part was carried up to Olympus. There he was made a god, made peace with Hera at last, and married her daughter Hebe, goddess of youth.

IX
Part IX · 6 chapters

Theseus and Crete

A hero of Athens, a king of Crete, a genius inventor and a monster in a maze.

Chapter 46

Minos and the bull from the sea

Minos, son of Zeus and Europa, wanted to prove that the gods favoured him as king of Crete. He prayed to Poseidon to send a bull from the sea, which he promised to sacrifice. A magnificent white bull rose from the waves. Minos thought it was too beautiful to kill, and sacrificed another bull in its place.

Poseidon took revenge. He made Minos’s wife, Pasiphae, fall madly in love with the bull. She asked the inventor Daedalus for help, and he built her a hollow wooden cow. The child she bore was the Minotaur, a man with the head of a bull, who ate human flesh.

Chapter 47

Daedalus and the Labyrinth

Daedalus was the greatest inventor of his age, an Athenian exiled for killing his own nephew, who had threatened to outshine him. For Minos he built the Labyrinth, a maze so complex that even its builder could barely find his way out. The Minotaur was shut inside.

Minos had a son, Androgeos, who was killed in Athens. To punish the Athenians, Minos demanded a tribute: every nine years, seven young men and seven young women were to be sent to Crete and fed to the Minotaur.

Chapter 48

Theseus on the road

Theseus was the son of Aethra, a princess of Troezen, and of either King Aegeus of Athens or Poseidon. Aegeus had left a sword and sandals under a heavy rock, saying that when the boy could lift it, he should come to Athens.

At sixteen Theseus lifted the rock and set out for Athens by land, along a road full of bandits. He dealt with each in the way they dealt with travellers. Sinis, who tied people to bent pine trees and let them spring apart. Sciron, who made travellers wash his feet and then kicked them off a cliff to a giant turtle. And Procrustes, who made his guests fit his bed by stretching them or cutting off their legs.

In Athens, Aegeus’s wife, the sorceress Medea, recognised who Theseus was and tried to poison him. Just as he raised the cup, Aegeus saw the sword at his side, knew his son and knocked the poison away.

Chapter 49

Into the Labyrinth

When the time of the tribute came, Theseus volunteered to go to Crete as one of the fourteen. He promised his father that if he survived, his ship would come home with white sails instead of black.

In Crete, Minos’s daughter Ariadne fell in love with him. She gave him a ball of thread, a gift suggested by Daedalus. Theseus tied one end at the entrance of the Labyrinth and unwound it as he went. In the heart of the maze he found the Minotaur and killed it with his bare hands, or with a sword Ariadne had smuggled to him. Then he followed the thread back out.

Chapter 50

The black sails

Theseus sailed away with Ariadne and the young Athenians. On the island of Naxos, he left Ariadne behind while she slept, perhaps on the gods’ orders. The god Dionysus found her there, made her his wife, and set her wedding crown in the sky as a constellation.

As Theseus approached Athens, he forgot to change the sails. Aegeus, watching from the cliffs, saw the black sails and believed his son was dead. He threw himself into the sea, which has been called the Aegean ever since. Theseus became king of Athens, united the villages of Attica into one city, and ruled it well.

Chapter 51

Icarus

Minos was furious that Daedalus had helped Theseus, and locked him in the Labyrinth with his young son Icarus, or kept them prisoners on Crete. Daedalus could not escape by land or sea, so he took to the air.

He collected feathers and fixed them together with thread and wax into two pairs of wings. He warned his son: “Fly the middle way. Too low, and the sea spray will soak your wings. Too high, and the sun will melt the wax.”

They flew out over the sea. But Icarus, thrilled to be flying, climbed higher and higher. The sun softened the wax, the feathers came loose, and he fell into the sea and drowned. That sea is still called the Icarian Sea. Daedalus flew on alone to Sicily, where King Cocalus protected him, and where Minos, still hunting him, was killed in his bath.

X
Part X · 8 chapters

Jason and the Argonauts

The greatest crew of heroes ever assembled sailed to the end of the world for a golden fleece.

Chapter 52

The golden ram

King Athamas of Boeotia had two children, Phrixus and Helle, by his first wife. Their stepmother hated them, and arranged for a false oracle demanding Phrixus’s death. Just as he was about to be sacrificed, a ram with a fleece of pure gold, sent by the gods, flew down and carried both children away.

As they flew over the sea, Helle slipped from the ram’s back and fell. That strait is still called the Hellespont, “Helle’s sea”. Phrixus reached Colchis, at the far eastern end of the Black Sea. He sacrificed the ram to Zeus and gave its golden fleece to King Aeetes, who hung it on an oak in a sacred grove, guarded by a dragon that never slept.

Chapter 53

The man with one sandal

In Iolcus, Pelias had taken the throne from his half-brother Aeson. Aeson’s son, Jason, was smuggled away as a baby and raised by the wise centaur Chiron. An oracle had warned Pelias to beware a man wearing one sandal.

When Jason was grown, he came to claim the throne. On the way, he carried an old woman across a flooded river and lost a sandal in the mud. The old woman was Hera, and from then on she was his protector.

Pelias saw the one sandal and was afraid. He agreed to hand over the throne, but only if Jason brought him the Golden Fleece from Colchis, a voyage from which no one was expected to return.

Chapter 54

The voyage of the Argo

Jason had a ship built, the Argo, with a beam from the oracle of Dodona that could speak. He gathered the greatest heroes of the age: Heracles, Orpheus the singer, the twins Castor and Polydeuces, Peleus (father of Achilles), the winged sons of the North Wind, and many more. They were called the Argonauts.

On Lemnos they found an island of women who had killed all their men, and stayed there a long time. They lost Heracles when his companion Hylas was pulled into a spring by water nymphs, and Heracles would not leave without him. They freed the blind prophet Phineus from the Harpies, winged monsters that stole his food. In return, he told them how to pass the Clashing Rocks, which smashed together on any ship that tried to sail between them.

At the rocks they released a dove. The rocks crashed shut on its tail feathers, and as they opened again, the Argonauts rowed with all their strength. They made it through with only the tip of the stern broken. The rocks stayed still forever after.

Chapter 55

Medea

In Colchis, King Aeetes promised the fleece if Jason could do the impossible: yoke two fire-breathing bronze bulls, plough a field, and sow it with dragon’s teeth.

Hera and Aphrodite made Aeetes’s daughter Medea, a powerful sorceress, fall in love with Jason. She gave him an ointment that protected him from fire. Jason yoked the bulls and sowed the teeth, and armed warriors sprang from the ground. As Medea had told him, he threw a stone among them, and they turned on each other until all were dead.

Aeetes still would not keep his word. So Medea led Jason into the grove at night, sang the dragon to sleep, and Jason took the Golden Fleece.

Chapter 56

The long way home

The Argonauts fled with Medea, and Aeetes chased them. In the darkest version of the story, Medea killed her own young brother and scattered his body in the sea, so that her father had to stop to gather the pieces.

Their journey home took them up great rivers, past Circe’s island, through the Sirens (Orpheus sang so beautifully that the crew did not hear them), and across the deserts of Libya, carrying the Argo on their shoulders. Near Crete, the bronze giant Talos threw boulders at them, until Medea’s magic made him pull out the bronze nail in his ankle that held in his lifeblood.

Chapter 57

Medea’s revenge

In Iolcus, Medea tricked the daughters of Pelias. She cut up an old ram, boiled it in a pot with herbs and pulled out a young lamb. The daughters did the same to their father, and Pelias died. Jason and Medea were driven out and went to Corinth.

There they lived for ten years and had children. Then Jason abandoned Medea to marry the king of Corinth’s daughter. Medea sent the bride a poisoned robe and crown, which burned her and her father to death. Then, to take everything from Jason, she killed their own children, and escaped in a chariot pulled by dragons, sent by her grandfather the Sun.

Jason lived on, alone and unhappy. One day, as he sat in the shade of the rotting Argo, a beam fell and killed him.

Chapter 58

Orpheus and Eurydice

Orpheus, the Argonaut, was the greatest musician who ever lived. When he played his lyre, wild animals followed him, trees bent towards him and rivers stopped to listen.

He married the nymph Eurydice, but on their wedding day she stepped on a snake and died. Orpheus went down into the underworld to bring her back. He played for Hades and Persephone, and for the first time the Furies wept. Hades agreed to let Eurydice go, on one condition: Orpheus must walk ahead of her and not look back until they reached the world above.

They climbed through the darkness. Orpheus could not hear her footsteps. At the very edge of the light, he turned to make sure she was there. Eurydice was pulled back into the dark, and was lost forever.

Orpheus wandered in grief and would not look at another woman. In the end, he was torn apart by the Maenads, the wild women who followed Dionysus. His head floated down the river, still singing.

Chapter 59

The Calydonian boar

In the same generation, Artemis sent a giant boar to ravage the land of Calydon, because its king had forgotten to sacrifice to her. Prince Meleager called together many of the heroes who had sailed on the Argo to hunt it.

Among them was Atalanta, a huntress who had been raised by a bear and could outrun any man. She drew the first blood, and Meleager killed the boar and gave her the hide. His uncles were outraged that a woman should get the prize, and in the fight that followed, Meleager killed them.

At his birth, the Fates had said Meleager would live only as long as a log in the fire. His mother had snatched it from the flames and hidden it. Now, in grief for her brothers, she threw it back into the fire, and Meleager died.

Atalanta later refused to marry anyone who could not beat her in a race. Hippomenes won by dropping three golden apples from Aphrodite, which she stopped to pick up.

XI
Part XI · 8 chapters

The House of Thebes

A city founded with dragon’s teeth, and a family the gods would not leave in peace.

Chapter 60

Cadmus and the dragon’s teeth

Cadmus, Europa’s brother, never found his sister. The oracle at Delphi told him to stop looking, follow a cow, and build a city where it lay down.

The cow lay down in Boeotia. When Cadmus sent his men to a spring for water to make a sacrifice, they were killed by a dragon sacred to Ares. Cadmus killed the dragon, and Athena told him to sow its teeth in the ground. Armed men sprang up. Cadmus threw a stone among them, and they fought until only five were left. These five helped him build the city of Thebes, and became its noble families.

Cadmus married Harmonia, the daughter of Ares and Aphrodite, and all the gods came to the wedding. But Harmonia’s necklace, a gift made by Hephaestus, carried a curse, and it brought misfortune to everyone who owned it.

Chapter 61

The birth of Dionysus

Cadmus’s daughter Semele was loved by Zeus. Hera, disguised as Semele’s old nurse, persuaded her to ask Zeus to show himself in his true form. Zeus had sworn to grant her any wish. He came as lightning and thunder, and Semele was burned to ashes.

Zeus saved the unborn child and sewed him into his own thigh until he was ready to be born. This was Dionysus, “twice-born”, god of wine, theatre, wild joy and madness. He grew up hidden from Hera, then travelled the world, teaching people to grow vines, with a crowd of satyrs and Maenads behind him.

Chapter 62

Pentheus

When Dionysus came home to Thebes, his cousin Pentheus was king. Pentheus refused to accept that Dionysus was a god and tried to stop the women of Thebes from joining his wild rites on the mountain.

Dionysus led Pentheus, half-mad, up the mountain dressed as a woman to spy on the rites. The Maenads, in their frenzy, thought he was a wild animal. Led by his own mother, Agave, they tore him apart. Agave came back to Thebes carrying her son’s head, believing it was a lion’s. When the madness left her, she saw the truth.

Chapter 63

Oedipus

Laius, a later king of Thebes, was warned by the oracle that his son would kill him and marry his wife. When his wife Jocasta gave birth to a boy, Laius pinned the baby’s ankles together and had him left on a mountainside to die.

The shepherd who was told to leave him took pity on him and gave him to the childless king and queen of Corinth. They named him Oedipus, “swollen foot”, and raised him as their own.

As a young man, Oedipus heard the same prophecy at Delphi: he would kill his father and marry his mother. Horrified, he fled Corinth so that he could never harm the parents he loved. At a place where three roads met, he quarrelled with an old man in a chariot and killed him. It was Laius.

Chapter 64

The riddle of the Sphinx

Thebes was being terrorised by the Sphinx, a monster with the body of a lion, the wings of a bird and the face of a woman. She asked everyone who passed a riddle, and ate those who could not answer: “What walks on four legs in the morning, two legs at noon and three legs in the evening?”

Oedipus answered: “A human being. A baby crawls on all fours, an adult walks on two legs, and an old person walks with a stick.” The Sphinx threw herself from her rock and died.

Thebes made Oedipus its king, and he married the widowed queen, Jocasta. They had four children: Eteocles, Polynices, Antigone and Ismene.

Chapter 65

The truth comes out

Years later, a plague struck Thebes. The oracle said it would only end when the killer of Laius was found and driven out. Oedipus swore to find him, and the more he searched, the closer the truth came. The old prophet Tiresias told him he was the killer. A messenger from Corinth revealed that he had been adopted. And the old shepherd confirmed who his parents really were.

Jocasta hanged herself. Oedipus took the gold pins from her dress and blinded himself. He left Thebes as a beggar, led by his daughter Antigone, and finally found peace at Colonus near Athens, where the gods called him away and Theseus alone saw how he died.

Chapter 66

The Seven Against Thebes

Oedipus had cursed his sons, Eteocles and Polynices. They agreed to rule Thebes in turns, a year each. But at the end of his year, Eteocles refused to give up the throne.

Polynices raised an army in Argos, led by seven champions, one for each of the seven gates of Thebes. All but one were killed. At the seventh gate, Eteocles and Polynices met and killed each other, as their father’s curse had said.

Chapter 67

Antigone

Their uncle Creon became king. He ordered that Eteocles be buried with honour, and that the body of Polynices, the traitor, be left on the field for the dogs and birds. Anyone who buried him would be put to death.

Antigone believed that the laws of the gods were higher than the laws of kings. She covered her brother’s body with earth. Creon had her walled up alive in a tomb. Too late, the prophet Tiresias convinced Creon he was wrong. When he opened the tomb, Antigone had hanged herself. His son Haemon, who loved her, killed himself beside her, and Creon’s wife killed herself when she heard.

Ten years later, the sons of the Seven, called the Epigoni, attacked Thebes again, and this time they took it. Thebes was destroyed, and the age of its great heroes was over.

XII
Part XII · 11 chapters

The Trojan War

One apple, three goddesses and a prince’s choice set the whole world at war.

Chapter 68

The golden apple

The sea goddess Thetis was destined to have a son greater than his father, so Zeus married her to a mortal, the hero Peleus. All the gods were invited to the wedding except Eris, goddess of strife.

Eris came anyway. She rolled a golden apple into the middle of the feast, with three words on it: “For the fairest”. Hera, Athena and Aphrodite each claimed it. Zeus wisely refused to judge, and sent the three goddesses to Mount Ida, near Troy, where a young shepherd would decide.

Chapter 69

The judgment of Paris

The shepherd was Paris, a prince of Troy. Before he was born, his mother Hecuba had dreamed she gave birth to a burning torch, a sign that he would destroy his city. So he had been left on the mountain as a baby, and raised by shepherds.

Each goddess offered him a bribe. Hera offered power over all of Asia. Athena offered wisdom and victory in war. Aphrodite offered the most beautiful woman in the world. Paris gave the apple to Aphrodite.

The most beautiful woman in the world was Helen, daughter of Zeus and Leda, and she was already married, to Menelaus, king of Sparta.

Chapter 70

The oath of the suitors

When Helen was young, every prince in Greece had wanted to marry her. Her stepfather feared that whoever he chose, the others would start a war. Odysseus, the cleverest of them, suggested a solution: every suitor must swear to defend Helen’s chosen husband if anyone ever took her from him. They all swore, and Helen chose Menelaus.

Paris, now recognised as a prince, visited Sparta. Menelaus welcomed him as a guest. While Menelaus was away, Paris left with Helen, and with treasure from the palace. Whether she was taken or went willingly, the stories do not agree.

Chapter 71

The ships gather

Menelaus called on the suitors to keep their oath. His brother Agamemnon, king of Mycenae and the most powerful ruler in Greece, led the army. A thousand ships gathered.

Not everyone wanted to go. Odysseus pretended to be mad, ploughing a field with an ox and a donkey and sowing it with salt. The Greeks placed his baby son in front of the plough, and Odysseus swerved, proving he was sane. Achilles’ mother, Thetis, knew he would die young if he went to Troy, so she hid him disguised as a girl among the daughters of a king. Odysseus came with gifts of jewellery and weapons, sounded a war trumpet, and Achilles grabbed a spear.

Chapter 72

Iphigenia at Aulis

The fleet gathered at Aulis, but the winds would not blow. Agamemnon had offended Artemis, and the prophet Calchas said the only way to calm her was for Agamemnon to sacrifice his own daughter, Iphigenia.

Agamemnon sent for her, saying she was to marry Achilles. When she arrived, she was led to the altar. In some versions, Artemis swept her away at the last moment and left a deer in her place. In others, she died. Either way, the winds blew, the fleet sailed for Troy, and Agamemnon’s wife, Clytemnestra, never forgave him.

Chapter 73

Nine years of war

Troy had strong walls, built by Apollo and Poseidon, and great defenders, above all Hector, Paris’s eldest brother. The Greeks could not take the city. For nine years they camped on the beach and raided the towns around it.

The gods took sides. Hera, Athena and Poseidon helped the Greeks. Aphrodite, Apollo and Ares helped the Trojans. Zeus tried to stay above it all, and to follow what Fate had decided.

Chapter 74

The wrath of Achilles

In the tenth year, Agamemnon took a captive woman, Briseis, away from Achilles. Achilles, insulted, refused to fight any more. His mother Thetis begged Zeus to let the Trojans win until the Greeks gave Achilles the honour he deserved. This is where Homer’s Iliad begins.

Without Achilles, the Greeks were driven back to their ships. Hector broke through their wall and set fire to a ship. Achilles’ closest friend, Patroclus, could not bear it. He borrowed Achilles’ armour and led his men into battle. The Trojans, thinking Achilles had returned, fled. But Patroclus went too far, and Hector killed him and took the armour.

Chapter 75

Achilles and Hector

Achilles’ grief was terrible. He made peace with Agamemnon, and Hephaestus forged him new armour and a great shield showing the whole world. He returned to battle and killed Trojans until the river was choked with bodies.

The Trojans fled inside the walls. Only Hector stayed outside to face Achilles. At the last moment his courage failed, and he ran. Achilles chased him three times around the walls of Troy. Then Athena tricked Hector into standing to fight, and Achilles killed him with a spear through the throat.

For days, Achilles dragged Hector’s body behind his chariot. Then, at night, Hector’s old father, King Priam, came alone into the Greek camp, knelt and kissed the hands that had killed his son, and begged for his body. Achilles thought of his own father, and the two enemies wept together. He gave back the body, and the Trojans buried Hector, tamer of horses. Here the Iliad ends.

Chapter 76

The death of Achilles

Achilles fought on. He killed Penthesilea, queen of the Amazons, and Memnon, king of Ethiopia, who had come to help Troy. But his own death was near.

When Achilles was a baby, Thetis had dipped him in the river Styx to make him invulnerable, holding him by the heel. That heel was the only place he could be wounded. Paris, guided by Apollo, shot an arrow that struck it, and Achilles, the greatest of the Greeks, died.

His armour was to go to the bravest Greek left. Ajax and Odysseus both claimed it, and the Greeks chose Odysseus. Ajax went mad with shame and fell on his own sword.

Chapter 77

The wooden horse

Prophets told the Greeks what they still needed to win, and they found it: the bow of Heracles, carried by Philoctetes, who shot Paris. Achilles’ son, Neoptolemus. And the Palladium, a sacred statue of Athena, stolen from inside Troy by Odysseus and Diomedes.

Then Odysseus came up with a plan. The Greeks built a huge wooden horse, hid their best warriors inside, and sailed away out of sight, leaving one man behind, Sinon, who told the Trojans that the horse was an offering to Athena. If they brought it into their city, Troy could never fall.

The priest Laocoon warned the Trojans: “I fear the Greeks, even when they bring gifts.” He threw a spear at the horse. Two great serpents came out of the sea and crushed him and his sons. The Trojans took it as a sign, pulled down part of their walls, and dragged the horse inside. Cassandra, Priam’s daughter, cursed by Apollo always to tell the truth and never be believed, warned them too. No one listened.

Chapter 78

The fall of Troy

That night, while the Trojans slept after their celebrations, the Greek warriors climbed out of the horse and opened the gates. The fleet had come back in the dark.

Troy burned. Neoptolemus killed King Priam at the altar of Zeus. Hector’s baby son was thrown from the walls. Cassandra was dragged from Athena’s temple. The Trojan women were shared out as slaves. Menelaus found Helen and meant to kill her, but when he saw her, he dropped his sword.

A Trojan prince, Aeneas, son of Aphrodite, escaped from the burning city, carrying his old father on his back and leading his young son by the hand. After ten years, Troy was gone.

XIII
Part XIII · 12 chapters

The Homecomings

Winning the war was easier than getting home from it.

Chapter 79

Angry gods

The Greeks had committed terrible crimes in Troy, desecrating temples and altars, and the gods were angry. Athena, who had helped them win, now sent storms against them. Many ships were wrecked. Others were blown far off course, and some of the heroes never saw home again.

Nestor, the wise old king of Pylos, got home quickly and safely. Menelaus and Helen wandered for eight years, as far as Egypt, before they returned to Sparta, where they lived on in peace together.

Chapter 80

The return of Agamemnon

Agamemnon came home to Mycenae in triumph, with Cassandra as his captive. His wife Clytemnestra welcomed him warmly and laid out a purple carpet for him to walk on.

Clytemnestra had never forgiven him for Iphigenia, and while he was away she had taken a lover, Aegisthus. When Agamemnon stepped into his bath, she threw a net over him and killed him with an axe. She killed Cassandra too, who had foreseen it all and gone inside anyway.

Chapter 81

Orestes and the Furies

Agamemnon’s son Orestes had been sent away as a boy. When he grew up, Apollo ordered him to avenge his father. With the help of his sister Electra, he came home in secret and killed Aegisthus, and then his own mother.

The Furies, who hunt those who shed their family’s blood, pursued Orestes across Greece and drove him mad. At last he came to Athens, where Athena set up the first court with a human jury to judge him. The votes were equal. Athena cast the deciding vote, and Orestes was set free.

The Furies were given a new home and a new name: the Eumenides, “the kindly ones”. With that, the chain of revenge in the house of Atreus was finally broken, and justice in Athens began to belong to the courts.

Chapter 82

Odysseus: the Cyclops

Odysseus, king of the small island of Ithaca, took ten years to get home. This is the story of Homer’s Odyssey.

After Troy, his twelve ships came to the land of the Cyclopes, one-eyed giants. Odysseus and twelve men went into a cave full of cheese and sheep, and waited for its owner. He was Polyphemus, a son of Poseidon. He rolled a huge boulder across the entrance, and ate two of the men for supper.

Odysseus told the giant his name was “Nobody”. He got Polyphemus drunk on strong wine, and when he fell asleep, the men drove a sharpened, red-hot stake into his single eye. Polyphemus screamed for help, but when the other Cyclopes asked who was hurting him, he shouted “Nobody!”, and they went away. In the morning, the men escaped by clinging to the bellies of the sheep as the blind giant let them out to graze.

As he sailed away, Odysseus could not resist shouting his real name. Polyphemus prayed to his father Poseidon for revenge, and Poseidon heard him.

Chapter 83

The winds and the witch

Aeolus, keeper of the winds, gave Odysseus a leather bag with all the storm winds tied up inside, so that a gentle breeze could carry him home. Within sight of Ithaca, while Odysseus slept, his crew opened the bag, thinking it held gold. The winds burst out and blew them all the way back.

The giant cannibal Laestrygonians smashed eleven of his twelve ships with boulders. With the last ship, Odysseus reached the island of the witch Circe, who turned half his men into pigs. Hermes gave Odysseus a magic herb called moly that protected him from her spells. He forced Circe to turn his men back, and they stayed with her for a year.

Chapter 84

The land of the dead

Circe told Odysseus he must visit the underworld to ask the dead prophet Tiresias how to get home. At the edge of the world, he dug a pit and filled it with the blood of sacrificed sheep, and the ghosts came crowding up to drink.

He spoke with Tiresias, who warned him never to harm the cattle of the Sun. He saw his own mother, who had died of grief waiting for him, and tried three times to hug her, but she slipped through his arms like a shadow. He met Agamemnon, who told him how he had been murdered, and Achilles, who said he would rather be a poor farmer’s slave on earth than king of all the dead.

Chapter 85

Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis

The Sirens sang so sweetly that sailors who heard them steered onto the rocks. Odysseus filled his men’s ears with wax and had himself tied to the mast, so that he could hear the song and live. He begged to be untied. His men only tied the ropes tighter.

Next came a narrow strait. On one side was Scylla, a monster with six heads on long necks, who snatched sailors from passing ships. On the other was Charybdis, a whirlpool that swallowed the sea three times a day. Odysseus steered closer to Scylla, and lost six men instead of the whole ship.

Chapter 86

The cattle of the Sun

They landed on the island of Thrinacia, where the cattle of Helios grazed. Odysseus made his crew swear not to touch them. But storms kept them on the island for a month, their food ran out, and while Odysseus slept, the starving men killed some of the cattle and ate them.

When they sailed again, Zeus struck the ship with a thunderbolt. Everyone drowned except Odysseus, who clung to the wreckage and drifted for nine days.

Chapter 87

Calypso and the Phaeacians

He washed up on the island of the nymph Calypso. She loved him and kept him there for seven years, offering to make him immortal if he would stay. Odysseus sat on the shore every day and wept for home. At last Athena persuaded Zeus to send Hermes, and Calypso had to let him go on a raft.

Poseidon wrecked the raft. Odysseus swam to the land of the Phaeacians, where the princess Nausicaa found him on the beach. At her father’s palace he told the story of his wanderings. The Phaeacians, great sailors, carried him home to Ithaca in a single night, and left him sleeping on the shore.

Chapter 88

The suitors

Odysseus had been gone for twenty years. In his palace, over a hundred young nobles were courting his wife Penelope, eating his food and drinking his wine, and plotting to kill his son Telemachus.

Penelope had held them off for years. She told them she would choose a husband when she had finished weaving a burial shroud for Odysseus’s father. Every day she wove, and every night she secretly unpicked her work. After three years, a maid gave her away.

Athena disguised Odysseus as an old beggar. Only his old dog, Argos, knew him. The dog wagged his tail, and then died, having waited twenty years to see his master.

Chapter 89

The bow of Odysseus

Penelope announced a contest. She would marry the man who could string Odysseus’s great bow and shoot an arrow through the holes of twelve axe heads lined up in a row.

One by one, the suitors tried to string the bow, and none of them could even bend it. Then the beggar asked for a turn. He strung it easily, plucked the string so that it sang, and shot an arrow straight through all twelve axes. Then he threw off his rags and turned the bow on the suitors. With Telemachus and two loyal servants, he killed every one of them.

Penelope still had to be sure. She told a servant to move their bed out of the bedroom. Odysseus cried out that it could not be moved: he had built it himself around the trunk of a living olive tree. Only her husband could know that. Penelope ran to him at last, and they wept in each other’s arms.

Chapter 90

Aeneas

Not every survivor of Troy was Greek. Aeneas, the Trojan prince, sailed west with the last of his people, looking for a new home. Roman poets, above all Virgil, later told his story in the Aeneid.

On the way, he loved and then abandoned Dido, the queen of Carthage, who killed herself in grief. He visited the underworld and saw the future of the city his descendants would build. At last he reached Italy, fought for a place there, and founded the line of kings that led to Romulus and Remus, and to Rome. The story of Troy did not end with its fall. It became the beginning of another empire.

XIV
Part XIV · 5 chapters

The End of the Age of Heroes

Greek mythology has no doomsday. Instead, the heroes fade, and history begins.

Chapter 91

The death of Odysseus

Tiresias had told Odysseus that after he came home, he must set out once more, walking inland with an oar on his shoulder until he met people who had never seen the sea and thought the oar was a winnowing fan. There he should sacrifice to Poseidon, and then death would come to him gently, from the sea, in a comfortable old age.

Later poets told a stranger ending. Telegonus, Odysseus’s son by Circe, grew up and went looking for his father. He landed on Ithaca, was taken for a raider, and in the fight that followed, killed Odysseus without knowing who he was, with a spear tipped with the spine of a stingray. Death had come from the sea after all.

Chapter 92

Why the heroes were ended

Ancient poets gave a reason for the end of the Age of Heroes. The earth, they said, was weighed down by the number of people on it. Zeus, with Themis, planned the Theban and Trojan wars to thin out the race of demigods.

Hesiod says that when the heroes died, Zeus took some of them to the Isles of the Blessed, at the edge of the world. There they live without sorrow, with Cronus, released from Tartarus at last, as their king, and the earth bears fruit for them three times a year.

Chapter 93

The Age of Iron

After the heroes came the people of the Age of Iron. That, said Hesiod, is us. We work hard by day and worry by night. Parents and children quarrel, guests and hosts cheat each other, and promises are broken.

Hesiod warned that this age too would end, when babies are born with grey hair and shame and justice leave the earth for good. Then Zeus will destroy this race of humans as he destroyed the others. But that ending is a warning, not a story the Greeks ever told. Unlike the Norse, with their final battle of the gods, Greek mythology has no end of the world.

Chapter 94

The gods step back

After Troy, the gods walk the earth less and less often. They no longer sit at the tables of kings or fight beside heroes on the battlefield. They speak through oracles, signs and dreams instead.

Mythology gives way to history. The heirs of the Trojan War heroes become the ancestors of real Greek families and cities. The Dorians come down into Greece. The first Olympic Games are held, in 776 BC, by the traditional count, and the Greeks begin to count their years by them. The age of stories becomes the age of records.

Chapter 95

How the stories survived

The gods of Olympus were worshipped for more than a thousand years. Their stories survived because poets, playwrights and storytellers kept retelling them.

There was never one official version. Homer and Hesiod, around the 8th to 7th centuries BC, sang the oldest stories we have. The tragedians of Athens, Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, put the heroes on stage. Later writers, such as Apollodorus in Greek and Ovid and Virgil in Latin, collected the myths and wove them together. They often disagreed, and many myths exist in several different versions. This retelling follows the best-known one, and mentions others along the way.

The myths still shape how we speak. We call a weak spot an Achilles’ heel, a source of trouble a Pandora’s box, a long journey an odyssey, a hard task Herculean, and a person in love with themselves a narcissist. Atlas holds up our maps, Nike puts wings on our shoes, and the planets still carry the gods’ Roman names. Three thousand years later, the stories are still being told.
